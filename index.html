Does jumping help kids grow taller?

Kids jump all day long. Researchers have tested what that impact does to growing bones, and whether it has anything to do with height. Here's a clear look at what they found.

Read the full article

Every landing sends a message to the bone

Bone is living tissue that keeps adapting to the loads it carries. When a child lands from a jump, the ground pushes back with a force that can reach several times their body weight. Bone cells sense that strain and respond by adding mineral, gradually making the bone denser. The hips and lower spine, which absorb the most impact, tend to change the most.

Children respond to this more strongly than adults. Their skeletons are still developing, so regular impact has a bigger effect during the growing years.

What studies show about bone strength

A seven-month jumping program
In a randomized trial of 89 children, mostly ages 6 to 9, one group did about 100 jumps off a low box three times a week. Compared with a stretching group, they built significantly more bone at the hip and lower spine.
Twelve trials pointing the same way
A 2025 review of 12 controlled studies with 940 children and teens found that high-impact jumping built more bone than low-impact exercise or resistance training alone.
Benefits that last for years
Children who completed a short jumping program early in life still had stronger hip bones nearly eight years later.

What about height?

Denser bone and longer bone are separate outcomes. In the seven-month trial, children in both groups grew by about the same amount.

A 2025 study found something different in one specific group. Over 24 weeks, 47 children with short stature, ages 8 to 11, followed either a jumping program or their usual routine.

Control group ~2.1–2.5 cm

Stronger hip bone appeared to explain part of the difference. The researchers describe this as early evidence limited to this population, and they call for larger, longer trials before applying it to children in general.

Growth plates set the window

Bones grow longer at growth plates, bands of cartilage near the ends of long bones. They stay open through childhood and the teen years, then fuse, usually around ages 16 to 18 in girls and 18 to 21 in boys. After that, bone length is fixed.

While the plates are open, jumping supports healthy bone development. After they close, it still builds bone density and fitness, but it no longer affects height. Genetics sets the broad range of adult height, and healthy habits help a child grow well within it.

Practical tips for parents

  • Keep it moderate. The studies used short sessions a few times a week. Much more volume adds strain without adding benefit.
  • Let play count. Jump rope, hopscotch, basketball, and trampoline time all provide the kind of impact researchers studied.
  • Support bones with food. Calcium, vitamin D, and protein give bones what they need to respond to activity.
  • Teach soft landings. Bent knees and controlled landings matter more than jump height or rep counts.
  • Protect sleep. Rest, balanced meals, and daily movement work together during the growing years.
